HOOKED ON A FEELING

Michael Rock / 2015

“There has long been an ideological divide between the utilitarian and the emotional, between rational design and the decorative arts, but the balance of power is shifting:

In the battle between the head and the gut, the gut’s now cleaning up.

The empathy economy is booming. Facts are out, feelings are in.”

In Hooked on a Feeling, Rock challenges the idea that design is purely meant for problem solving. Instead, he argues that emotion plays a crucial role in how we experience design. I agree with this because I see this theory in action every day. Most of the products I use in my daily life create feelings: happiness, comfort, confidence, fun, or relief. Even products that seem purely functional are often successful because they generate some sort of feeling in the user.

“We designers love to fashion ourselves as “problem solvers,” but let’s face it: the line between solving problems and creating them is blurry at best. What problem is being solved by a $1,500 pair of heels, or a $150,000 car, or a $150,000,000 architectural extravaganza in the Bois de Boulogne? The truth is, no matter what designers say, we have never simply answered problems. The act of design vacillates between the functional and the beguiling, often performing one while claiming the other.”

“functionality is boring; performance is about what role the building plays and what kind of scene it triggers.”

Quick personal tangent: Emotion in the “boring” Products I Use This made me think about the digital products I use every day to get work done and be productive. Google Maps helps me get where I need to go, Google calendar helps me be organized with events and tasks, my Notes app helps me remember things I may forget. At first these products may seem straightforward as if they are there to solve these simple problems and add functionality to my life. But they also make me feel productive, confident, and clear-headed. I trust them to do their jobs, and that makes my life less stressful. A calendar that technically organizes my schedule but makes me feel overwhelmed is not well designed. A navigation app that consistently gives the wrong directions does not feel reliable. So I would say that, yes, emotion is one of the most important factors that determines why, how, and if a product is successful. However, I don’t think that functionality and feeling are opposites. Instead, I think that feeling has become part of whether a functional solution actually works.

Rock also writes,

“So if the goal of design is emotional coherence, how is that end achieved? Enter the mood board: the most quotidian, slightly-embarrassing and now ubiquitous design tool.”

I found this interesting because mood boards are something designers use as a part of the process throughout the early stages of designing a product. But he describes them as not only collections of aesthetic photos together to guide a vision but something that helps designers to communicate and establish a feeling that can be difficult to describe through words. This brought to my attention how much of interaction design is about creating an atmosphere of emotion. Every little detail like color, typography, motion, imagery, sound, language can make the experience elicit a completely unique emotion in the user.

“As the longing for emotional connection spreads from how we want our clothes or living room to feel, to how we want our minivan or pharmaceutical or constitutional democracy to feel, the mood business continues to expand. (A friend recently told me her therapist encouraged her to make a mood board expressing the feelings related to her recent divorce.) This expansion is fueled, in large part, by the sheer overabundance of available images. It’s hard to remember that a couple of decades ago, finding pictures of things was actually quite arduous and involved countless hours of random page flipping. Now a few seconds of furious key-stroking produces endless examples to regurgitate. Every possible idea is immediately diluted by retroactive references. That glut of images is constantly resifted through personal curation tools such as Pinterest and Tumblr, and inspiration websites such as ffffound.com or vfiles.com. Such hyper-individualistic online collections, in turn, become open-source mood repositories scavenged by branding professionals. Why assemble collections of your own when there are millions that exist ready for appropriation? Need to evoke cozy softness with a vaguely synthetic overtone, an early seventies color palette, and just a touch of glam rock? Missile silo chic? Mid-century Crimean beach culture? I can guarantee someone out there has that covered. There are even Pinterest pages dedicated to collecting other mood boards: collections of collections. And then, of course, there is Instagram, which turns every individual life into a social network mood board. The carefully curated Instagram account is essentially a personal image management tool: private life as public brand. While the ascendency of mood boarding reflects the way brands, from commercial products to people, are imagined at this moment, the question remains why we are all so susceptible to manufactured emotion. Why are we so needy? Perhaps this yearning is a reaction to the lurking presence of Big Data. As vast files of meta-data and personal search histories are ferreted away in some server farm in god-knows-where, we crave the kind of anodyne, gauzy experiences that at least promise something warmer and more human. When everything is available all the time and we’re inundated with information in every way, shape and form, we’re left no choice but to favor what makes us feel.” Published in T Magazine © Michael Rock

I also noticed after reading this article that it was written in 2015, so I read it again through a different lens. This time I had more context for how much technology has changed since the article was written and how much the focus has shifted. With the introduction of AI, efficiency, data and automation have become central to how we measure a product’s success. Or so it seems. AI can now generate experiences and interfaces extremely quickly, but that primarily benefits the function and efficiency of a product. It makes me wonder if that makes our role as designers even more important as facilitators of human emotion in an increasingly mechanical world.  Another question was also raised for me: if we are responsible for designing a user’s emotions, how should that responsibility be used? If we can intentionally create feelings that genuinely improve a user experience, we can also manipulate that experience in harmful ways. The ethical line becomes important. The goal for a designer cannot be simply to make users feel something, but also to consider whether that feeling is serving the user or the company.

Ultimately, besides raising several other tangential questions that I still don’t have answers to, my biggest takeaway from reading Hooked on a feeling was that designing a solution is not only about asking “Does this solve the problem?” It is also about asking, “What does it feel like to have this problem solved?” I think those two questions are much more connected than they initially appear.
