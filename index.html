Response to Hooked On A Feeling By Michael Rock

In Hooked on a Feeling, Rock challenges the idea that design is purely meant for problem solving. Instead, he argues that emotion plays a crucial role in how we experience design. I agree with this because I see this theory in action every day. Most of the products I use in my daily life create feelings: happiness, comfort, confidence, fun, or relief. Even products that seem purely functional are often successful because they generate some sort of feeling in the user.

Quick personal tangent: Emotion in the “boring” Products I Use

This made me think about the digital products I use every day to get work done and be productive. Google Maps helps me get where I need to go, Google calendar helps me be organized with events and tasks, my Notes app helps me remember things I may forget. At first these products may seem straightforward as if they are there to solve these simple problems and add functionality to my life. But they also make me feel productive, confident, and clear-headed. I trust them to do their jobs, and that makes my life less stressful. A calendar that technically organizes my schedule but makes me feel overwhelmed is not well designed. A navigation app that consistently gives the wrong directions does not feel reliable. So I would say that, yes, emotion is one of the most important factors that determines why, how, and if a product is successful. However, I don’t think that functionality and feeling are opposites. Instead, I think that feeling has become part of whether a functional solution actually works.

Rock also writes,

“So if the goal of design is emotional coherence, how is that end achieved? Enter the mood board: the most quotidian, slightly-embarrassing and now ubiquitous design tool.”

I found this interesting because mood boards are something designers use as a part of the process throughout the early stages of designing a product. But he describes them as not only collections of aesthetic photos together to guide a vision  but something that helps designers to communicate and establish a feeling that can be difficult to describe through words. This brought to my attention how much of interaction design is about creating an atmosphere of emotion. Every little detail like color, typography, motion, imagery, sound, language can make the experience elicit a completely unique emotion in the user.  I also noticed after reading this article that it was written in 2015, so I read it again through a different lens. This time I had more context for how much technology has changed since the article was written and how much the focus has shifted. With the introduction of AI, efficiency, data and automation have become central to how we measure a product’s success. Or so it seems. AI can now generate experiences and interfaces extremely quickly, but that primarily benefits the function and efficiency of a product. It makes me wonder if that makes our role as designers even more important as facilitators of human emotion in an increasingly mechanical world.  Another question was also raised for me: if we are responsible for designing a user’s emotions, how should that responsibility be used? If we can intentionally create feelings that genuinely improve a user experience, we can also manipulate that experience in harmful ways. The ethical line becomes important. The goal for a designer cannot be simply to make users feel something, but also to consider whether that feeling is serving the user or the company.  Ultimately, besides raising several other tangential questions that I still don’t have answers to, my biggest takeaway from reading Hooked on a feeling was that designing a solution is not only about asking “Does this solve the problem?” It is also about asking, “What does it feel like to have this problem solved?” I think those two questions are much more connected than they initially appear.
